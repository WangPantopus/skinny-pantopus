The feature

In one minute

  1. Report.The owner adds a photo, the pet's name and kind, and where and when it was last seen. About a minute.
  2. Alert.People whose home or saved place is within about a mile get one push. Quiet hours hold it until morning.
  3. Spread.A print-ready flyer with a QR code, and one link for Facebook groups, Nextdoor and texts. Every channel opens the same pet page.
  4. Sightings.Anyone can tap "I saw them" and drop a pin, with no app and no account. The household sees each one arrive on a map.
  5. Home.The owner marks the pet home. Everyone who helped hears it, and the alert ends.

The case

Why this, of everything we could add

  • It's the one moment people want every stranger within a mile to hear from them. That is the audience a neighborhood app can't buy. Each flyer puts a Pantopus QR code on poles in the right streets, and each Facebook or Nextdoor post links back to a Pantopus page.
  • It helps the owner with nobody nearby on Pantopus. The flyer, the link and the sightings page work on day one. The push gets better as neighbors join.
  • Strangers meet Pantopus by helping, without signing up. The page then offers the reason to install: "Get alerts for lost pets near you."
  • It fits the rules we already keep. The feed refuses crime and suspicion heads-ups. This is the cooperative opposite, and a pet coming home is the best news a street shares.

How it should feel

Eight principles

A minute from panic to posted

Ask for a photo, a name, the kind of pet, and where and when. Everything else can wait.

One page, every channel

The push, the flyer's QR code, the Facebook post and the text message all open the same pet page.

Anyone can help

Seeing the page and reporting a sighting needs no app and no account.

The home stays private

The last-seen spot shows as an area, never the house. The owner's phone number stays off the page unless they add it.

Calm, not alarming

One push per pet, only to people nearby, never during quiet hours, with a daily and weekly cap.

The assistant suggests, people decide

A photo match says "might be", and only the household sees it.

Every alert ends

Home, closed by the owner, or expired, and the people who helped hear how it ended.

Useful alone, better with neighbors

Nothing in the owner's flow depends on anyone nearby already using Pantopus.

Built in from the first release

Privacy and safety

  • The home stays private. The last-seen spot uses the area rule post pins already follow: snapped to a grid, then moved by about 500 m for everyone but the household. The page names a nearby public place, such as a park or an intersection. Today a post keeps whatever place name the poster typed; the alert writes its own label instead.
  • Photo location data is removed. Post photos already lose their location data on upload. The alert takes photos only, and refuses an upload when stripping fails instead of keeping the original, as the upload path does today.
  • No names on the public page. It shows the pet's name and "Biscuit's family", never the owner's name, handle or picture. The pet page gets its own endpoint that returns safe fields only, as the Plans preview does.
  • Phone numbers stay private by default. The pet page and the flyer show a phone number only when the owner adds one.
  • Scam warnings where scammers strike. The pet page, the flyer and the first message from someone new all say: never pay anyone or read out a code before your pet is home.
  • Fake alerts. A photo is required, and it gets an automatic check (an animal; no people, no house numbers) before the push goes out. If the check fails or can't run, the alert still posts and can be shared, and the push waits for a review. A report hides the alert for review, as post reports do today.
  • Prank sightings. Limits per device and per alert, links removed from notes, and the household can hide a sighting and mute that device. Pantopus has no captcha anywhere today; add one only if abuse shows up.
  • Reporters' privacy. A reporter's exact pin goes to the household only. Contact details are optional, seen by the household only, and deleted 30 days after the alert ends. Sighting photos are stored privately, not under the public post-media path.
  • Reach counts. "About 140 people" is rounded and never shown below 10, like the density meter.
  • Search engines. An open alert's page may be indexed, so someone searching "lost dog" nearby can find it. It drops out of search when the alert ends.

Draft, for the build brief

Data and endpoints

Two new tables (D12)

  • PetAlert, one per lost or found pet, linked to its Post (the public card, as with Plans) and to a HomePet when there is one. Kind, pet name, species, description, last seen at, the true last-seen point (household only) and its public label, reach, status (open, home, not home, closed, expired), push times, short code, end date.
  • PetSighting: the alert, seen at, the true point (household only) and its area, a note, a private photo, the reporter's account if any, optional private contact, a device hash for limits, and when it was hidden.

Additive, no new tables

  • A lost_pets switch on notification preferences.
  • A database function that lists people with a home or saved place inside a radius.
  • The pet_alerts flag row, funnel event names, and /pet/* in the iOS link file.

Endpoints

  • POST /api/pet-alerts creates the post and the alert, then queues the photo check and the push.
  • GET /api/pet-alerts/:id household view (exact) or neighbor view (area).
  • PATCH /api/pet-alerts/:id edits, moves the last-seen spot, extends, or ends it.
  • GET /api/pet-alerts/:id/sightings exact for the household, area for everyone else.
  • PATCH /api/pet-alerts/:id/sightings/:sid hides one and can mute that device.
  • GET /api/public/pets/:code no account; safe fields only, like the Plans preview.
  • POST /api/public/pets/:code/sightings no account; when, where, and an optional note, photo and contact; strict limits.

Web pages and jobs

  • Pages: /pet/:code, its "I saw them" form, and /pet/:code/flyer.
  • Jobs: the nearby push (reach, caps, held pushes), the photo check, photo matching, the day-before notice and expiry, and the 30-day cleanup.
  • Funnel: alert created, flyer opened, link shared, page viewed, sighting sent, app clicked, home.

Neighbors with an account message the household in the existing chat. People without one reach the household through the sighting form. If they leave an email, Pantopus sends them one message only: the "home" news, with a link to get alerts nearby.

How we'll know

Success signals

The bars are first guesses, to adjust after the pilot.

  • Speed. Median time from opening the form to "Send alert": under two minutes.
  • Spread. Alerts whose flyer was opened or whose link was shared: most of them.
  • Reach outside the app. Pet-page views from people without an account, per alert. This is the acquisition engine.
  • Help. Alerts with at least one sighting.
  • Growth. Sign-ups that start on a pet page, and how many of them come back in week four.
  • Attention cost. People who turn off "Lost pets near me" within a day of an alert: kept rare.
  • Outcome. Alerts marked Home (self-reported, so read it as a trend).

Said plainly

Limits and risks

  • It won't fill Nearby by itself. A neighborhood may see a few alerts a month. This brings people in; Today, Place and Plans have to keep them.
  • Early alerts reach almost nobody by push. The flyer and the link carry the first months; the push grows with density.
  • Others already do lost pets. PawBoost, Petco Love Lost, Facebook groups and Nextdoor. The difference is one tap to all of it, and a page that works for people who never install anything.
  • Some pets don't come home. "Found, but not coming home" closes quietly: no celebration card and no push to helpers, only a note if the owner writes one.
  • Abuse will be tried. Fake alerts, prank sightings and reward scams. The rules above cover the known ones; watch reports closely in the pilot.

Why: Waking a street for a pet is the fastest way to get notifications turned off for good. The owner's flyer, link and pinned card work at any hour, and Plans already holds pushes this way.

Why: The person who sees the dog is usually not on Pantopus yet, and every required step loses sightings. The page ends with "Get alerts for lost pets near you", the moment most likely to win a download.

D7

Open

Why: Area and time are enough to send searchers the right way, and a reporter who spotted the dog from their porch doesn't publish their own address.

Open

How do people reach the owner, and can there be a reward?

Why: Scammers watch lost-pet posts for phone numbers and rewards: "I have your dog, send the reward first", or "read me the code we just texted you".

D9

Photo matching

Open

Should Pantopus compare photos of found pets with missing ones?

Why: Words like "tan dog" match half the neighborhood; photos are what people compare. Today's pairing also reaches only people who turned on Suggestions, which most owners never will. A check costs a few cents, inside the assistant's existing daily budgets.

D10

Found pets

Open

Does a found pet alert neighbors too?

Why: Many owners aren't on Pantopus. A finder's alert reaches neighbors who may know the dog, and the finder's page works for an owner without an account.

D11

Open

Can households save their pets before anything happens?

Why: The pets list already exists on web, iOS and Android and holds a photo, species, breed and microchip number. A saved pet turns the report into one tap and gives pet owners a reason to set up the app before anything happens. Doing it second keeps the first release on the moment of loss.
